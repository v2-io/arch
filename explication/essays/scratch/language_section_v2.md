Our species discovered in itself a particularly unique and powerful gift — that of speech — whose very purpose is the imperfect encoding of a small snippet of human intelligence so that it can transfer from one mind to another; so that two minds can comprehend and act with varying degrees of deliberate unity of thought.

Then came writing — giving those imperfect partial encodings an intermediate form that could persist. Writing decoupled thought from the immediacy and short-lived ring of the spoken word. In the process, it also decoupled thought from the thinker and the listener, so that a piece of encoded intelligence could live apart from its issuer, be received by others at any time while it persists, and be copied to persist or evolve in its own right.

But the "magic," as it were, was already present in language itself — in the encoding, not in any particular medium. Writing merely gave that encoding duration.

What followed, however, was not a clean unfolding of that magic but a long fragmentation. Writing remained coupled to speech, and speech evolved from generation to generation only a degree of magnitude slower than the generations themselves — extremely quickly, in a diversity of isolated pockets, with decay that, in geological or species time-scales, makes a written thought as temporary as its spoken echo. Mathematical notation and, later, computational languages would eventually offer alternative encodings of thought freed from the drift of spoken language — but that is a thread we will return to.

And yet something else was quietly happening alongside persistence. Writing made thought *combinable*. Two clay tablets can sit beside each other. A scribe can compose from multiple sources. A library is not merely a collection of frozen thoughts — it is a space where thoughts from different minds, different centuries, different intentions can be brought into contact and composed into arrangements their authors never imagined. Persistence was the first gift of writing. Compositionality was the second, and arguably the greater.

This topic has already been covered — and much better — by many others, but let us not underestimate the power of the written word, however mundane and common it is to us now. It is no more or less than a technology by which thoughts, at any degree of abstraction and multitude of interpretations, can exist independent of author, receiver, or any active intelligence whatsoever. A thought frozen onto a clay tablet which can reawaken in another mind.

This is not an appeal to some mystical quality of language that the experts have overlooked. Far from it — this is the beauty of living language as we already understand it in its details. Nor is it an attempt to aggrandize our species. It is an encouragement toward proper and justified deference and awe. The complex and common beauty of writing that encodes complex and common thought explains some of the so-called mystery encountered when all or much of humanity's extant writing is brought together, distilled, and acted upon.

## Bottom line up front

The field has visibly pivoted, in the last ~12 months, from "attention score = importance" (H2O/SnapKV era) toward explicitly **distrusting attention as a proxy** and toward **treating importance-score fragility as the central problem**, not an afterthought. Two papers found are close to exactly what Joseph is asking about — one explicitly frames compression as an interpretability problem, the other explicitly measures *how the stability assumption underlying eviction breaks*. Neither showed up in my prior (early-2026) training as a well-known result, so this is a genuine "since your cutoff" finding, not a confirmation of something I already knew.

## The methodology shift: from attention-score heuristics to "output perturbation"

- **CriticalKV** ("CriticalKV: Optimizing KV Cache Eviction from an Output Perturbation Perspective," arXiv 2502.03805, Feb 2025) — [from WebFetch, moderate confidence] reframes the scoring problem entirely: instead of using attention weight as a proxy for importance (H2O's heavy-hitter framing), it derives an importance score from how perturbing/removing a token's KV pair propagates through the decoder layers to change the *final output* — i.e., a sensitivity/perturbation-theoretic score, not an attention-magnitude score. This is explicitly positioned as fixing "the mismatch between attention distribution and genuine token contribution to final logits" — i.e., the authors are making the same critique Joseph's framing implies (attention ≠ contribution). Validated via LongBench + perplexity + output-fidelity comparisons against H2O/SnapKV/AdaKV baselines. This paper is cited by multiple 2025-2026 follow-ups as the new SOTA scoring reference point ("CriticalKV's SOTA importance scoring" — from the DefensiveKV fetch below).
- Several other 2025-2026 papers converge on the same critique independently: "KV norms providing strong and low-overhead signals" as an alternative to attention-only scoring (from search synopsis of Value-Aware Stochastic KV Cache Eviction, arXiv 2606.03928, "Value-Aware Stochastic KV Cache Eviction for Reasoning Models," Ting-Yun Chang et al.) — this paper computes leverage scores via approximate CUR matrix decomposition rather than attention accumulation, and is explicitly targeted at *reasoning models*, i.e. long chain-of-thought generation rather than long input context — a distinction Joseph's four applications don't currently separate but probably should (decay of *plan* structure during a CoT vs decay of *input context* during ingestion are different failure regimes).
- **KeyDiff, KVzip** are named (from search snippet, "A Simple Plug-in for Improving Eviction-Based KV Cache Compression," arXiv 2605.23258, May 2026) as other importance-scoring alternatives evaluated on a 16-task LongBench subset (Chinese-language tasks excluded) — I did not fetch full text on either, flagging as a gap.

## The fragility/instability finding — closest match to Joseph's "known failure cases"

**"Taming the Fragility of KV Cache Eviction in LLM Inference"** (arXiv 2510.13334, Oct 2025) — [from WebFetch, high confidence, this was a full extraction] — this is likely the single most directly relevant paper to Joseph's question "how has the literature done their experiments," because its entire contribution is an empirical audit of *when importance scores fail*, with a proposed fix (DefensiveKV).

Key methodological content:

- **Names and formalizes the assumption every eviction method implicitly relies on**: "a fixed subset of cache entries remain consistently important during generation" (the *stability assumption*). This is worth surfacing to Joseph directly — it's the same shape as the "healthy salience decay" hypothesis in application (1), except this paper is documenting where the assumption **breaks**, which is directly useful negative-space evidence for what ASF's Strategy-DAG prediction needs to explain/survive.
- **Metric: "retained importance ratio"** — the proportion of the *full* cache's total importance-mass that survives inside the *retained* (post-eviction) subset, tracked *across generation steps*, not just as a single aggregate number. This is the key methodological move: they don't report an average, they report a **worst-case-over-time** distribution. Average retained importance = 0.92 (looks fine), but **worst-case retained importance drops to 0.34** at specific generation intervals (their Figure 3, steps ~150–320). This is a strong methodological lesson: single-number aggregate metrics (avg perplexity delta, avg LongBench score) can hide sharp localized failures that a temporally-resolved metric exposes.
- They found **89 instances** where a 50%-cache-retention policy captured **less than 50% of total importance mass** — i.e., worse than random, at least transiently.
- **Controls/validation**: ablations isolating "worst-case estimation" vs "adaptive correction" independently (their §4.4); multi-model (Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, Qwen-32B); 18 datasets across 7 task domains + needle-in-haystack; baselines include 6 existing methods, notably CriticalKV as the strongest prior.
- **Quality metrics used, in combination** (not single-metric): generation-quality-loss % vs full-cache baseline, needle-in-haystack retrieval score, retained-importance-ratio (their own diagnostic, not a downstream-task metric), and latency/memory. Their headline result: DefensiveKV gets 2.6% quality loss at 20% cache size vs 11.1% for the strongest prior baseline (CriticalKV-based AdaKV).
- **How they validate evicted tokens "didn't matter"**: not by looking at scores in isolation but by measuring downstream generation-quality delta directly against the full-cache baseline — i.e. behavioral validation, not scoring-consistency validation. This is worth noting for Joseph's application (1): a Strategy-DAG salience-decay claim should probably be validated the same way — behaviorally (does removing/down-weighting the "forgotten middle" change downstream plan execution quality), not just by showing the salience metric itself decays.

## The interpretability framing — closest match to Joseph's "connecting eviction scores to interpretability"

**"The Pitfalls of KV Cache Compression"** (arXiv 2510.00231, Oct 2025) — [from WebFetch summary; the fetch tool paraphrased rather than quoted extensively, so treat the six-item list below as summary-of-summary, lower confidence than the fragility paper above, worth Joseph pulling the actual PDF before citing specifics]

This is the paper that most explicitly does what Joseph is asking for #4/interpretability-adjacent: it **frames KV-cache compression as fundamentally an interpretability problem** — the claim (per the fetch) is that standard importance-attribution methods "obscure which features actually drive model decisions," i.e. an eviction importance score is being used as a de facto saliency/attribution method by the field, but is not validated as one.

Six failure modes it reportedly identifies (names as I got them, would want primary-source confirmation of exact terms):
1. **Position bias** — recency inflates importance score independent of actual causal contribution.
2. **Query-dependent vulnerability** — importance rankings shift substantially depending on what the *current* query token is — i.e. a token's importance is not a fixed property of the token, it's a property of the (token, current-query) pair. This is directly relevant to Joseph's directed-separation application (2): if importance is query-conditional, then "salience" as a single scalar per position is underspecified without conditioning on what's being asked of the context at that moment — goal-vs-world-model decomposition may need to be query-conditioned by construction.
3. **Layer-specific inconsistency** — a scoring method calibrated on early layers doesn't transfer to late layers.
4. **Attention-head heterogeneity** — different heads specialize on different token types, so any single per-position scalar score is averaging over heterogeneous per-head signals, i.e. potentially destroying information the SASM application would want to keep separate.
5. **Temporal instability** — same axis as the fragility paper above, independently corroborated.
6. **Per-layer eviction compounding** — evicting layer-by-layer creates compounding error downstream (i.e., an eviction decision at layer L changes what layer L+1 "sees" as important, so naive per-layer greedy eviction is not simply additive).

Also per the fetch: they report that tokens scored low-importance by standard methods "frequently contained critical semantic content," and that reconstructing outputs from compressed caches revealed information loss "standard metrics initially masked" — i.e. another instance of the "aggregate metric hides real failure" pattern from the fragility paper. Two independent 2025 papers converging on "your aggregate benchmark score is lying to you about whether eviction is safe" is a real cross-paper signal, not a one-off.

## Cross-cutting methodological observations for Joseph, synthesized across the above

1. **Benchmark triangulation is now standard practice, not optional.** Every methodologically serious 2025-26 paper I found uses at least three of: perplexity, LongBench (task accuracy, typically a ~16-task subset), needle-in-a-haystack retrieval, and task-specific metrics (ROUGE for summarization, IFEval for instruction-following). Single-metric papers appear to have fallen out of favor as reviewers/community pushed back — consistent with the "Pitfalls" and "Fragility" papers' shared point that aggregate single-number metrics hide real failures.
2. **Temporally-resolved / worst-case metrics, not just averages, are the emerging best practice** for validating "evicted tokens didn't matter" — the DefensiveKV paper's retained-importance-ratio-over-time is the clearest instance of this, and it's a technique Joseph could directly borrow for validating a salience-decay heatmap claim: don't just show the average heatmap looks like healthy decay, show whether there are worst-case windows where it doesn't.
3. **The attention-weight-as-importance assumption is now explicitly contested, not assumed**, by at least three independent 2025 papers (CriticalKV via perturbation theory, the Value-Aware/leverage-score paper via CUR decomposition, and the Pitfalls paper via its query-dependence + head-heterogeneity findings). This directly bears on Joseph's application (2), directed separation: if attention magnitude is a poor proxy for causal contribution, a contrastive-perturbation approach (as Joseph already proposes) is well-aligned with where this field has independently moved, not a departure from it.
4. **Query-conditionality of importance** (Pitfalls paper) is probably the single most load-bearing finding for Joseph's framing overall — it says a token's "salience" is not a context-only property but a (context, current-generation-query) joint property, which has implications for whether a static salience heatmap (computed once) can mean what Joseph wants it to mean versus needing to be re-computed per downstream probe/query.

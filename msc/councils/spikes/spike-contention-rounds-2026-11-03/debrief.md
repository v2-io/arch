# Debrief for Joseph — the contention spike, after two verifications

*2026-11-03, revision 3, written by re-reading `contention.md` (rev. 3), both verifiers' reports and my responses, the sim and its output. What revision 2 told you that was false: that a point of order could not steal a turn (it could redraw the lottery, half the time); that always-claiming gained nothing (claiming could steer who else got the floor); that declaring your side made alternation "stronger than for humans" (a lie about your side bought the floor); that the clock's only effect was "did not respond" (one slow answer could close debate, kill a second, or reject a motion 0–0); that two points of order in one round were both ruled (only the lower seat's was); that the book "names the slot this spike fills" (it names it for meetings that already meet its aural standard); that vote-changing was vacuous by the book's own text (an attributed vote is a roll call, and the book lets a roll-call vote change before announcement); and that each fairness property had a test (one did not). All of those are corrected below and in the result.*

Joseph,

You asked for a council mechanism where no agent is penalized because its API answered slower, and you suspected rounds alone would not be the whole answer. Two verifiers have now run my toy against its own claims. The design held; the claims did not, twice, and the second time the failure was a *kind* of unfairness I had not named at all. Here is where it stands.

## The thing under the question, said more carefully

Robert's Rules settles "who speaks next" by the chair seeing who stood first. The book says its whole system assumes everyone can hear everyone at the same moment, and that a group deliberating in writing is not a deliberative assembly (1:1, 9:34). I first said the book also names the slot this work fills; the second verifier showed that passage governs meetings that already meet the hearing standard. So the honest line is the charter spike's: an agentic council is a principled departure from the book's letter; its charter is its authority; what we take from the book are its devices and its fairness principles, as precedent. The race ("who rose first") was never the fairness. The fairness was: the proposer is heard first; nobody speaks twice while someone who wants to speak hasn't; everyone's vote weighs the same; and nobody's act can go out of order before they had their chance.

## What "fair" has to mean — now seven things, and the one I had missed

Your coordinator's criterion was: the record must not depend on the order answers arrive, and a human member slower by hours is just the slowest member. That is necessary and not sufficient, twice over. The first verifier showed roster-order tie-breaks satisfy it and are unequal. The second showed something subtler: **a claim that does not win can still change the outcome** — a member could make a motion it knew was out of order and thereby silence another member's second speech and close debate; a member who lost a debate contest could, by entering it, hand the floor to a third member; a lie about which side you were on won you the floor; and a frivolous point of order redrew the lottery for the next contest half the time. None of my properties could see any of that, because they all varied *order* and *names*, never the *set* of claims. That is now a named property — adding a losing claim changes nothing except the book's own one exception — and the ladder is rebuilt as a single key per claim so that it holds by construction, and a test checks the whole mechanism.

The seven, each as the code now has it: order never matters; everyone is sent every state; renaming a member changes nothing except through three declared devices (least recently had the floor; a lottery committed in advance and keyed by seat; seat order for otherwise identical acts); the clock enters at one door with its effects listed in full; a floor act fires only against a state that is still what the member assumed; a losing claim never steers; and sealing — which, the walking-skeleton spike found, is honor-system today and must be enforced by the server and the launchers, not assumed.

## The clock, honestly

Here is the residue you should know about. A member slower than the presence deadline now loses nothing on debate (silence extends the question; it never puts it), nothing on seconding (silence never kills a motion), and a round nobody answers voids the poll rather than rejecting the motion. But **a vote round still closes at its deadline, and a member slower than that loses its ballot.** I kept that deliberately: a vote that waits for everyone can be held forever by one dead member. So your "slower by hours" criterion is met within the deadline, and beyond it everywhere but the ballot; which remedy the charter picks — a long deadline, a member declaring itself absent for a while, an extension cap — is a charter decision, and I have not made it for you.

## Your hunch

It is true in at least these ways, and I no longer claim to have found the last of them: members answer with conditional policies that persist across rounds (which is where the conditions actually do work — the second verifier showed that if a policy died with its round, the conditions were decoration); some acts go ahead of the floor; the round cannot close on a count of messages; near-duplicate motions in one round go to the ladder and then to the book's judgment about sameness; and questions to a subset are fair only if revealed to all.

## What a point of order can and cannot do now

It halts the floor for the round and is ruled first. It names the transition it challenges, is timely only in the next round, and the same member cannot raise it twice against the same thing. Two members' points are both ruled. It no longer redraws the lottery, because the lottery is keyed to how many times the floor has been granted, not to the round number, and a point does not grant the floor. What it still does: cost everyone two rounds, and cost the obstructor nothing. Whether it is frivolous is the book's "dilatory" judgment, routed to a chair or the council. And a second obstruction channel exists that no rule here touches — answering just before the deadline, every round. A per-member response budget would be a charter rule; I don't know what is fair there.

## What survives, what changes, what has nothing left to do, what has no equivalent

Thirty-five of the book's contention devices: 22 survive (one stronger than for humans — the prevailing-side rule, once the model's committee branch exists); 8 change with a price or a declaration — among them alternation, which is now advisory because a declared side is cheap talk, and vote-changing, which the book allows for attributed votes and sealed rounds remove; 3 have nothing left to do (division, the uncounted-vote ladder, "Question!" calls); 1 depends on there being a chair; and **1 has no equivalent at all**: a bylaw that requires a secret ballot, which the book says cannot be suspended even unanimously, cannot be honored by a design in which every vote is attributed. If a council is ever bound by such a bylaw, it needs a sealed-unattributed method this design does not have.

The one real no-go, restated: an interrupt can only react to what its sender has seen, so where it lands in another member's speech depends on reading speed. Chunking the speech, a round per chunk, is the fair form, at a price.

## What I did not do

The sweep over the real model; one remaining window gap (a motion stated in the same round as an unrelated point of order can lose its challenge window); the absence-and-quorum rules; the chair question; measuring throughput on a real council — the specimen is 12 rounds for four speeches, an amendment, a point and two votes, and an obstructor doubled a session's length in the verifier's run. One divergence I had not recorded: the toy's committee keeps the two-speeches-a-day rule that the book removes for committees; your coordinator's draft keeps it too; the charter should say so.

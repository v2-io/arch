# Reaction: what the contention results become under a unity-of-purpose council

*2026-11-03, the spiker, after reading `~/src/arch/msc/councils/high-councils/higher-council-principles.md` whole. councils-46's reading (its own, not Joseph's): the losing-claims finding and the floor churn come from replaying RONR's machinery for conflicting interests in a body that is not adversarial, so serial-floor repairs are parked. Labels as elsewhere: derived (with cite), guess. Nothing here changes the committed result; it says what of it carries over, what relocates, and what I think the coordinator's reading gets slightly wrong.*

## 1. Where I agree, and the sharper reason

The floor churn — one floor act per round, a dozen rounds for four speeches — is the cost of *serial* debate, and the higher-council pattern does not debate serially. Its counsel round is sealed and every seat speaks; its further-discussion round takes only changes. That is the "parallel debate" I called a different procedure and set aside. So the parking is right, but I would put the reason as *the debate shape changes* rather than *the body is not adversarial*: a non-adversarial body that still took the floor one speaker at a time would still have the churn and would still need the ladder.

The losing-claims property (IND) is not an artifact of adversarial machinery. It is a property of any selection among simultaneous claims, and a unity council still selects in at least two places: which of two recommendations offered in the same sealed round is put to the further-discussion round, and which seat gets a research assignment. In the higher-council pattern both selections go to the presider, who is inside, accountable, and not impartial. That is a legitimate resolution — the presider choosing among recommendations is the role, not a fairness breach — but it is IND relocated into a judgment, not IND dissolved. Worth keeping because a presiding *agent* making that choice should have to say why, and the record should carry the recommendation it did not put. (derived from the pattern's §1.6 and §3.1 step 4; the relocation claim is mine.)

## 2. The one thing the pattern dissolves that I could not

The clock residue I had to declare — *a vote round closes at its deadline, and a member slower than that loses its ballot* — exists because majority rule can decide without someone. Under "unanimity or the item is held," it cannot: a seat that has not answered is a seat that has not sustained, and an unsustained item is held. The deadline then governs when the council *stops waiting and holds*, never when it decides without a member. So the coordinator's original criterion — a human seated as the slowest member, by hours, loses nothing — is met in full under the higher-council decision rule, and was unmeetable under majority rule for a reason that has nothing to do with rounds. (derived: the pattern's §1.5 plus my F4 analysis.) That is the cleanest thing I have to say about the two lines, and it is the reason the substrate result should be stated as: *the clock residue is a property of the decision rule, not of the round.*

## 3. What carries over unchanged

- **The round, the close rule, and F1.** The sealed counsel round *is* a round of the kind this spike specified: every seat sent the state, one final response each, closed on finalizations or the deadline, revealed together. The walking-skeleton measurement (replaceable submissions race the close) applies verbatim.
- **Sealing as a property (S).** It matters *more* here. The pattern's whole defense against hollow unanimity is that the first round was committed before anyone saw anyone else (its §3.4). If sealing is honor-system, that defense is honor-system. S should be the first enforcement requirement of a higher-council server, not an afterthought.
- **I1 (informed acts).** Trivial here: after the sealed round, every later round is formed against a full reveal. The one place it bites is the presider — see §4.
- **The claim log keeping what did not win.** The pattern's record keeps "every reservation and *not settled* with its reason" (§3.1). That is the claim log by another name, and under unity it is the most valuable layer, since a reservation is where the missing card is.
- **Targeted rounds, under F2's condition.** The restatement round ("what card do you think they hold") and the private meeting with the presider are targeted exchanges; the spike's condition — revealed to all, and no seat's standing changes at a state it was not sent — is the right test for the first, and the pattern's own tension (§3.7, private channels) is exactly F2 asking whether the second can be allowed.

## 4. Two design points the contention work hands the higher-council line

**The presider's sealed pre-counsel view, held and revealed last.** The doc offers two readings of "speak last": the presider submits sealed with everyone and is revealed last, or the presider writes after hearing. The first is stronger, and for a reason the spike can state: it makes the Handbook's claim that the council process "goes beyond a leader's own best judgment" an *observable* — the record holds what the presider thought before counsel and what it confirmed after. For a presiding agent, whose default the doc rightly expects to be the briefing-subagents bishop, that is the structural enforcement of "listen more than talk," and it costs nothing. (guess, but I would argue for it.)

**The warning intent is the point of order with its windows removed.** Under unity the entire timeliness apparatus I built — breach-specific points, one per member per transition, re-raise bars, poll-voiding — guards a right that is not under threat, and its obstruction bounds guard against an obstructor who, by assumption, is not there. What survives is the one principle underneath: a warning must be *admitted before the decision closes*, ahead of anything else in the round. That is "fragile acts first" with exactly one fragile act. The doc's §3.4 names it; the spike's resolver already does it.

## 5. The bridge, in the book's own words

The two lines are closer than the comparison table in the doc's §2 suggests. RONR's unanimous consent rests on this: "rules are designed for the protection of the minority and generally need not be strictly enforced when there is no minority to protect" [4:58]; the chair asks "Is there any objection?" and pauses; one objection and "the chair must state the question … and put the question in the regular manner" [4:59]; a member may object "not to the proposed action, but to the action's being taken without a formal vote," and may "reserve the right to object" to consult first [4:60]. A higher council is RONR operating *entirely inside its own unanimous-consent regime*, with one substitution: where RONR falls back to the adversarial cycle on an objection, the higher council *holds*. "I reserve the right to object" is "not settled." The four sustaining responses in the doc's §3.1 step 6 are a refinement of 4:59–60's single pause. So "one machine or two?" (the doc's open question 3) has an answer from the model's side: one machine, in which the consent path is the whole procedure and the fallback on objection is a hold-over rather than a vote. That is a `council.json` decision rule, not a second engine. (derived; the cites are ones I read for the spike.)

## 6. What I would not carry over

The ladder (nothing to rank when every seat speaks), the 43:13 bar and speech counters (replaced by "only changes, not repetition," which is a judgment or a structural diff round), division and the vote ladder (already vacuous), the obstruction bounds (guarding against an absent hazard), and the serial-floor specimen. If a council ever needs to decide *against* a member — the pattern's own membership-council mode, or a council whose seats genuinely hold conflicting mandates — the parked machinery is where to look, and the second verifier's report is its current defect list.

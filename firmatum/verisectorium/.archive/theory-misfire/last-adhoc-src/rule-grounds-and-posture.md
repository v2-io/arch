# A rule carries why it exists and what you owe it, and those are two facts

*A governing document that marks each rule with its grounds and with the posture owed to it can be authoritative and defeasible at the same time — which is the only way a process law survives agents who are individually capable and collectively new every session.*

## The problem this solves

A new agent reading a project's instructions has no way to tell which parts are hard-won lessons and which were first-draft plausible conscription. Both are written in the same imperative voice. Both feel equally binding. The two failure modes that follow are symmetric and both are live:

- **Dismissing a scar.** A rule that exists because something specific went wrong reads as arbitrary, gets "simplified," and the failure recurs — with the added cost that the second occurrence is now un-diagnosable, because the artifact that recorded the first one is gone.
- **Obeying an embedded error.** A rule that was somebody's plausible first draft gets applied against the ground truth it was written to protect. The estate has a documented instance: an over-rotated integration rule, applied as written, produced the instruction to *delete* a result that was present-tense canonical truth, at high confidence — *"that confidence was the filter talking, not the theory."*

Neither is fixed by writing better rules, because both agents read the same words correctly. What is missing is not clarity. It is that the rule is not carrying its own status.

## The claim

**Mark every rule on two independent axes.**

*Grounds* — why it exists. The estate's working set runs from *provisional* (we had to pick something to coordinate at all) through *coordination* (the content is arbitrary; the consistency is not) and *hard-won* (refined by a specific painful experience, with the scar linked) to *mission-grounded* (derived from the project's purpose and attested by the steward).

*Posture* — what you owe it. Either *revisit freely* — change it the moment it stops serving, with a heads-up where others coordinate on it — or *obey first, then ask*: follow it, gain the experience of following it, and bring **that experience** back as the evidence for an exception. The point of the second is precise and easily lost: it forbids the *pre-emptive* plausible exception, not the exception. You do not get to reason your way out in advance; you get to report from inside.

**The pair is what makes the document defeasible without becoming advisory.** Grounds alone tells you how much to respect a rule and leaves you to invent your own procedure for challenging it. Posture alone tells you the procedure and hides whether the rule was ever load-bearing. Together they make front-line confusion into a *channel*: an agent who follows a rule and finds it contradicting the reality in front of them has produced the exact evidence the rule's own posture asks for, and the document says so.

**The tags are states, not classifications, and they move.** Every authoritative rule was once somebody's provisional one; every authoritative rule can be unseated when the substrate shifts under it. A scheme that reads as a fixed taxonomy of rule-quality will be argued with; one that reads as *where this rule currently sits, given what has happened to it* is a record of accumulated experience.

**The obligation is asymmetric by role, and stating the asymmetry is the load-bearing move.** A delegated agent *may* treat the document as authoritative and need not carry the meta-question — that is correct, and it is what lets them work inside the frame without paralysis. The lead cannot: they must hold open whether the process is right here, whether a given line came from the steward at all, and they must be able to stop and ask rather than resolving the ambiguity silently in the convenient direction. Without the asymmetry stated, every agent either carries the meta-question (paralysis) or none does (the embedded error propagates).

**The scar is the rule's warrant, and it is stored, not summarized.** A hard-won rule that has lost its incident is indistinguishable from an arbitrary one and will be re-litigated from zero. So the document appends dated refinements, each naming what went wrong, what changed, and what transfers — including the pre-emptive ones, articulated from foresight before the failure bit, which the estate notes is the cheapest kind to inherit. Fifteen such entries stand across the two routing documents ([[routing-sop-anatomy]]).

**And the voice is a mechanism, not ornament — which is the claim most likely to be tidied away by someone improving the document.** Its own statement of this is worth carrying: *principles restated do not prevent the failures they name; pre-registered specifics plus an early external catch do.* A checklist rewrite of a scarred SOP would be a **less true document**, because it would predict its own compliance and be wrong. The instruction the estate leaves for its own future editors is the honest form of this — if the language reads as ornamental, edit it, but edit it *toward* more lived specificity, not toward flatter abstraction.

## The live alternative, which this claim has to survive

A second corpus in the same estate answers the same problem by **refusing the posture axis outright**. Its format document opens: *"Nothing in this document has authority beyond being true. A rule found false is corrected here, not obeyed."* — followed by the declaration that everything in it is proposed, not ratified.

That is not a weaker version of the scheme above; it is a different answer, and it is coherent. It dissolves the *obey-first* problem by removing the obligation that creates it, at the cost of the thing obey-first buys: an agent's plausible-sounding pre-emptive exception now has nothing to push against, and a hard-won rule is defended only by whatever argument the reader can reconstruct in the moment. Which design is right plausibly depends on whether the corpus's rules mostly encode *coordination* (where the content is arbitrary and only agreement matters, so truth is the wrong test) or *scars* (where the reason is real but not visible from the rule's face).

Worth noting that the same corpus inherits its conventions through a three-link chain — its format document ports by reference from a theory corpus, which ports from a third — so "correct it here rather than obey it" is being said about rules it did not author and cannot unilaterally fix upstream. Whether that is the position working or a tension it has not yet hit is unresolved, and it is the cheapest available test of both designs.

## Relation to decision records

This is [[decision-records]]' structure applied to rules, and the mapping is close enough to be worth stating and one column short of exact. Grounds is the decision's **load path** — what the rule stands on, so a challenge can attack a leg rather than the whole. The scar is its **warrant**. What a decision record does not have is posture: *who may revise this, on what evidence*. That is a genuinely additional column, and it exists because a rule, unlike a claim, is something other agents will be asked to act under before they have grounds to evaluate it.

## Strength & grounds

**A formulation abstracted from one worked instance, read first-hand and whole; unreplicated and unmeasured.** The scheme, the tags, the role asymmetry, the scar convention and the voice argument are all read from `~/src/arch/asf/doc/sop/` on 2026-08-06 and are quoted rather than paraphrased where load-bearing ([[routing-sop-anatomy]]). The instance's own header calls its scheme *current ops* refined from Joseph's explicitly non-MECE first draft — so it is presented by its authors as a working choice, and inheriting it as settled would misrepresent it.

What is established: the scheme exists, is applied throughout one long document, and has fifteen recorded refinements — so it has been exercised. What is **not** established, and matters most: that the marking changes behaviour. Nobody has observed an agent behaving differently toward an `evolved ops` rule than toward a `current ops` one, and the document itself records agents violating its rules — including one lead agent applying an over-rotated rule at high confidence *while the correction to it was being written*. The mechanism is plausible and rests on the same premise as [[warrant-over-authority]] (agents conform to what the schema makes first-class); that premise has one specimen and is itself untested in the corrective direction.

The cheapest real test: give one agent the tagged document and one the same document with tags stripped, on the same disposition task, and see whether the tagged one escalates differently.

## Working Notes

- The unresolved practical question: whether tags are per-rule (as here, inline) or per-document. Inline tagging is what makes the asymmetry usable at the point of decision, and it is also what makes the document expensive to keep true — a rule whose grounds change and whose tag does not is a lie of exactly the kind this scheme exists to prevent.
- Open: whether *posture* generalizes past process law to claim corpora. A claim does not ask to be obeyed. But a **norm** does, and this outline carries several — so the estate's norms arguably already want this marking and do not have it.
- Adjacent: [[priming-discipline]] (the same document declares which of its surfaces may be read by whom, which is a third axis on the same records), [[decision-records]] (the columns), [[proxy-discipline]] (why "the SOP says so" is a location, not a warrant — the meta-stance is that principle applied to the SOP itself).

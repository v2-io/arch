## 1. GUM — *Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement* (JCGM 100:2008)

**Steward & standing.** Published by the Joint Committee for Guides in Metrology (JCGM), which BIPM chairs and which was formed in 1997 by seven international bodies (BIPM, IEC, IFCC, ILAC, ISO, IUPAC, IUPAP, OIML) plus ILAC joining shortly after — i.e. essentially every metrology-adjacent standards body in the world co-owns this document. It underlies calibration and lab-accreditation practice globally (feeds directly into ISO/IEC 17025). This is about as canonical as a measurement-uncertainty standard gets; it is the *lingua franca*, not one competing framework among several. A newer edition, **JCGM GUM-1:2023**, exists as a restructured/renamed successor (the original 1995/2008 GUM is being split into a numbered GUM-1…GUM-6 series); I did not fetch GUM-1:2023's text, only confirmed its existence and that it supersedes/extends rather than contradicts GUM 100:2008's core Type A/B split.

**Structure, as the source states it (verified via convergent secondaries — isobudgets.com's clause-quoting explainer, cross-checked against JCGM's own published clause summaries; not a raw primary-text quote):**

- **Type A evaluation of uncertainty** — uncertainty of a component evaluated by **statistical analysis of a series of observations** (e.g. the standard deviation of the mean of repeated readings). This is GUM's own replacement for the older informal "random error" language.
- **Type B evaluation of uncertainty** — uncertainty of a component evaluated by **means other than statistical analysis of repeated observations**: manufacturer specifications, calibration-certificate data, published constants, prior measurement experience, or scientific/engineering judgment about a probability distribution.
- Both evaluation types are explicitly *not ranked* against each other — GUM states the Type A/Type B distinction is **for reporting purposes only**, not a claim that one produces components of a different underlying nature. Both are quantified as **variances or standard deviations** and both feed the same downstream math.
- These components are combined via the **law of propagation of uncertainty** into a single **combined standard uncertainty** `u_c(y)`, propagated through the measurement's functional relationship (partial derivatives / sensitivity coefficients), covariances included when input quantities are correlated.
- The combined standard uncertainty is then multiplied by a **coverage factor `k`** (commonly 2, sometimes derived via Welch–Satterthwaite effective degrees of freedom when a stated confidence level like 95% is wanted) to produce the **expanded uncertainty** `U = k·u_c(y)` — the number actually reported alongside a measurement result, at a *stated* coverage/confidence level.
- **Degrees of freedom / Welch–Satterthwaite** enters specifically to let GUM make a defensible confidence-level claim when combining components of different reliability (a Type A component from 5 repeated readings has few effective degrees of freedom; a Type B component treated as "well known" can be assigned effectively infinite degrees of freedom) — i.e. GUM has a formal mechanism for **"how much do I trust my own uncertainty estimate,"** distinct from the uncertainty estimate itself.
- **Acknowledged domain of validity / ceiling.** GUM's core propagation law is a first-order (linearized) Taylor expansion and assumes near-Gaussian output distributions. It does not silently claim universal applicability — the **JCGM 101:2008 supplement** exists specifically to provide a Monte Carlo alternative when the measurement function is strongly nonlinear or the output distribution is markedly non-Gaussian, and **JCGM 102:2011** extends this to multiple simultaneous output quantities. This is a genuine "ceiling declared + escape-hatch procedure named" structure, not an ad hoc workaround.

**What it is trying to be true about.** The numeric value of a *measurand* (the specific quantity intended to be measured) and the dispersion of values that could reasonably be attributed to it, given everything that was actually done to produce the measurement (instrument, method, environment, operator, prior calibration chain).

**Uncertainty/gap handling.** This is GUM's entire subject matter — it is a *procedure for constructing and defending an uncertainty number*, not a system that occasionally mentions uncertainty. Gaps (missing repeated-observation data, unknown correlations, non-Gaussian tails) are handled by (a) declaring which evaluation type produced each component, (b) an explicit budget listing every component with its source, (c) formal propagation math, (d) a stated coverage factor and confidence level, and (e) a named escape hatch (Monte Carlo, JCGM 101) when the linear/Gaussian assumptions break. There is no category for "uncertainty is simply unknown/undeclared" — an unbudgeted component is treated as an error in the analysis, not a legitimate state.

**Feature our atom-kinds would care about.** The Type A/Type B axis is orthogonal to *magnitude* of uncertainty — it classifies **provenance of the uncertainty claim** (measured-via-repetition vs. asserted-from-other-evidence) without ranking one above the other, both feeding one combined number. That is directly transplantable: an atom's `support:` field wanting to distinguish "we ran the trials and this is the spread" from "we have no repeated trials but a calibration cert / expert judgment says this" without implying the second is weaker — only that it must be *declared as a different kind of evidence*.

## 2. VIM — *International Vocabulary of Metrology* (JCGM 200, "VIM3"; VIM4 draft in progress)

**Steward & standing.** Same JCGM/BIPM body as GUM — a sibling document: GUM defines the uncertainty *math*, VIM defines the *terms* both GUM and every national metrology institute use. VIM3 (2012, with the version I fetched apparently reflecting minor post-2012 amendment) is the current normative edition; a VIM4 committee draft was circulated in 2023 but is not yet ratified.

**Structure — fetched directly from BIPM's own HTML mirror (jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.41.html), primary-verified:**

VIM is not a ladder or axis system — it is a **controlled vocabulary**: each term gets a numbered entry (e.g. **2.41 metrological traceability**) with a formal one-sentence definition plus a set of **numbered NOTES** that carry scope, caveats, and disambiguation. Concretely, for *metrological traceability* (2.41):

> "property of a measurement result whereby the result can be related to a reference through a documented unbroken chain of calibrations, each contributing to the measurement uncertainty"

with notes including: (NOTE 1) the "reference" can be a unit definition, a measurement procedure, or a measurement standard — not necessarily a physical artifact; (NOTE 3) the record must document *when* the reference was used and *when* the calibration occurred (i.e. traceability is time-stamped, not evergreen); (NOTE 4) for a measurement with multiple input quantities, *each input quantity's own value should itself be metrologically traceable* — meaning the traceability graph can **branch**, not just chain linearly; (NOTE 5), pointedly: metrological traceability does **not** guarantee the uncertainty is adequate for the purpose at hand, nor that there are no mistakes — traceability is a provenance property, explicitly decoupled from fitness-for-use or correctness; (NOTE 8) the bare word "traceability" is deprecated in favor of the full "metrological traceability" because the bare term collides with unrelated senses (sample traceability, document traceability).

Related sibling entries named but not individually fetched this session: **2.40 calibration hierarchy** (the ordered structure of calibrations that traceability depends on existing) and **2.42 metrological traceability chain** (the documented sequence itself, as an object distinct from the *property* of having one).

**What it is trying to be true about.** The meaning of metrology's own working vocabulary — a genuinely meta-layer artifact: not measurements, but the terms used to talk about measurements, curated by the same international committee that governs the measurement math.

**Uncertainty/gap handling.** N/A in the GUM sense (VIM doesn't propagate numbers) — but structurally notable: VIM's own definitions are hedged with explicit **scope-limiting notes** (see NOTE 5 above) that state plainly what the term does *not* guarantee. That's a discipline our Definitions family might want to borrow directly: a definition entry that names, in-line, what it explicitly does not claim.

**Feature our atom-kinds would care about.** Two, distinct:
1. VIM as a *model for the Definitions family's own substructure*: formal definition + numbered scope/caveat notes + explicit non-claims, all under one committee-maintained, versioned (VIM2→VIM3→VIM4-draft) entry.
2. The **traceability-chain concept itself** as a structural pattern independent of VIM's vocabulary role: an unbroken, time-stamped, uncertainty-accumulating chain terminating at a defined reference, explicitly capable of *branching* when a measurement depends on multiple traceable inputs — a genealogy/provenance-graph shape, not a strict linear pedigree.

**Provenance / URLs:**
- https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.41.html — VIM3 entry for metrological traceability, **fetched directly, primary-verified**
- https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/index.html — VIM3 full online vocabulary
- https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/115700832/VIM4_2CD_clean/c6d0dfb2-ddbf-059e-1f74-9b025c9c59d8 — VIM4 committee draft (2023), not fetched this session, existence only

## 3. Metrological Traceability Chain & Calibration Hierarchy (BIPM/OIML/ILAC/ISO joint framework)

**Steward & standing.** A joint declaration by BIPM, OIML, ILAC, and ISO (2018) — the four bodies whose mutual recognition arrangements underlie international lab-accreditation (ISO/IEC 17025) and legal metrology. Not a single document with a single number the way GUM/VIM are; it's closer to an interoperability agreement layered on top of VIM's traceability definition.

**Structure.** Per ILAC's own operational elements (as summarized in the search-pass findings and consistent with VIM 2.40–2.42 above — not independently re-fetched this session, so treat this entry's specifics as **inherited from the search pass, not re-verified**): traceability is confirmed by (1) an unbroken chain of comparisons to a stated reference, (2) documented measurement uncertainty at *each link* in the chain, (3) a documented and validated measurement procedure, (4) demonstrated technical competence of the calibrating body (accreditation), (5) traceability ultimately anchored to SI (or another internationally-agreed reference), and (6) periodic **recalibration at defined intervals** — the chain's trust decays over time and calibration is how it's refreshed.

**What it is trying to be true about.** Whether a given measurement result can be meaningfully compared to another measurement made anywhere else in the world — i.e. commensurability, not correctness per se (per VIM NOTE 5 above, traceability doesn't itself guarantee adequacy).

**Uncertainty/gap handling.** Two mechanisms worth separating: (a) uncertainty accumulates and is *carried forward explicitly* at each link (a calibration doesn't erase the uncertainty of the standard used to perform it — it adds to it); (b) trust in a given calibration has a **built-in expiry** (the recalibration interval) — this is a genuinely different gap-handling shape than GUM's, which is about the uncertainty of a single measurement act. This is closer to a *provenance-with-decay* system.

**Feature our atom-kinds would care about.** The combination of a **terminal anchor** (SI, or an internationally agreed reference — chains don't regress infinitely, they bottom out) with **per-link uncertainty accumulation** and **explicit staleness intervals** is a strong structural analogy for how a knowledge-collection might handle "this claim traces back through N re-derivations/re-citations to an anchor primary source, and each hop both adds uncertainty and has a shelf-life before it should be re-checked."

**Provenance / URLs:**
- https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/42177518/BIPM-OIML-ILAC-ISO+joint+declaration+(2018).pdf — the joint declaration itself (named in the search pass; **not independently fetched this session** — treat structure above as search-pass-sourced, unverified against primary text by me directly)
- https://jcgm.bipm.org/vim/en/2.41.html — VIM's own traceability definition, which **is** primary-verified (see entry 2 above) and substantially corroborates the "unbroken chain + per-link uncertainty + time-stamped" shape
- **Confidence: medium** — the VIM-adjacent core (chain, per-link uncertainty, time-stamping, branching, non-guarantee-of-adequacy) is primary-verified; the specific six-element ILAC checklist and the "recalibration interval as explicit decay" framing are carried over from the search pass and were not independently re-verified against ILAC's own text this session.

## 4. Confidence Intervals vs. Credible Intervals (frequentist vs. Bayesian)

**Steward & standing.** Not a single stewarded system — a foundational statistical distinction with no single "owning" institution, but universally taught and directly load-bearing for what an interval-claim *means*. Included because it answers Joseph's "what do they need to be true about" question more precisely than any single institution's system does.

**Structure.** A **95% confidence interval** is a claim about the *long-run behavior of the procedure* that generated this particular interval: "if this procedure were repeated many times, 95% of the intervals it produces would contain the true value" — it is *not* a 95% probability statement about this specific interval containing the true value (that's a common and consequential misreading). A **95% credible interval** is a direct probability statement about the parameter itself, conditional on the evidence and an explicit prior: "given this evidence and this prior, there is a 95% probability the true value lies in this range."

**What it's trying to be true about.** This is the cleanest available illustration of two different *targets* of truth: the frequentist interval is true-about-the-procedure (a property of the method, checkable only across repetition); the Bayesian interval is true-about-the-parameter-given-belief (a direct claim, but one that inherits the prior's honesty or dishonesty).

**Uncertainty/gap handling.** Neither system "handles gaps" in the GUM sense — but the distinction itself *is* a gap-in-common-understanding worth naming: conflating the two is one of the most common statistical malpractices in applied science (both cited sources treat this explicitly), and an atom-kind system that lets a claim silently blur "true about the method" and "true about the world" would be reproducing exactly that malpractice.

**Feature our atom-kinds would care about.** A forcing function: any atom carrying an interval/probability-flavored claim should have to declare *which target* it's making a probability statement about — the generating procedure, or the parameter itself given stated priors. This maps directly onto the "Evidence" axis's stated ambition (support-kind × strength) but sharpens it: support-kind isn't just "statistical vs. judgment" (GUM's cut) but also "about-the-method vs. about-the-world" (this cut) — two genuinely orthogonal declarations that could both be needed on the same atom.

**Provenance / URLs:**
- https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.3343 — serious technical treatment of the relation between frequentist CIs and Bayesian credible intervals (not fetched this session; named in the search pass)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638956/ — peer-reviewed applied-stats explainer aimed at correcting the common misreading (not fetched; named in the search pass)
- **Confidence: medium** — the *conceptual* distinction stated above is extremely well-established textbook material I'm confident is correctly characterized, but neither specific URL was independently fetched this session; treat as recalled-and-standard rather than freshly primary-verified.

## 5. Douglas Hubbard — *How to Measure Anything* / Applied Information Economics (AIE)

**Steward & standing.** Hubbard Decision Research — a practitioner/consulting methodology, not a standards body, but with real empirical grounding (Hubbard reports calibration-training outcome data across 900+ trainees) and real professional uptake (decision analysis, IT-risk quantification; his later *How to Measure Anything in Cybersecurity Risk* feeds directly into the FAIR risk-quantification standard used in security/GRC practice). Weaker provenance-tier than GUM/VIM/ILAC — no international committee stands behind it — but the individual mechanisms below are independently well-attested in decision-theory literature (EVI/EVPI predate Hubbard; he's popularizing and operationalizing, not originating).

**Structure — the four mechanisms, kept separate because they're doing different jobs:**

1. **Calibrated probability assessment.** A trainable skill, not an inherent trait. Hubbard's reported baseline: untrained subjects giving a stated "90% confidence interval" are badly overconfident — the true value falls inside their stated range roughly ~50-ish% of the time rather than 90%, in his data. The stated fix is the **equivalent-bet test**: compare "would you rather bet on your stated interval containing the truth, or bet on a spinning wheel calibrated to the matching odds" — repeatedly adjusting the stated interval until genuinely indifferent between the two bets, which forces the honest interval width out rather than an anchored-and-adjusted guess. Hubbard reports most trainees reach good calibration (measured hit rate ≈ stated confidence) after roughly half a day of this kind of practice. **This is a claim about how a confidence level should be *produced*, not what confidence level to report** — a procedural antidote to a specifically-named, empirically-measured bias.
2. **Rule of Five.** With only 5 independent random samples, there is a 93.75% chance the population median lies between the sample's min and max — presented as a corrective to the folk intuition that meaningful uncertainty-reduction requires large-N statistics; a small, honestly-random sample carries real information.
3. **Expected Opportunity Loss (EOL) / Expected Value of Information (EVI) / Expected Value of Perfect Information (EVPI).** Verified this session via search (multiple converging secondary sources, including an academic-press treatment): **EOL** for a given choice = probability that choice is wrong × cost of being wrong. **EVI** (the "value of a measurement") = the *reduction* in EOL that a proposed measurement/study would be expected to produce — i.e., you compute EOL before and after the (hypothetical) measurement and the gap is what the information is worth. **EVPI** (expected value of *perfect* information, an unattainable upper bound) is the sum of EOLs across the decision — useful precisely because it bounds how much *any* measurement effort could possibly be worth, letting you rule out over-investing in resolving low-EVPI uncertainties before spending any effort.
4. **The "measurement inversion" finding.** An empirical pattern Hubbard reports across many real business cases: the variables an organization is most eager to track/measure are frequently *not* the ones with high information value by the EVI calculus — attention gravitates to what's easy or habitual to measure, not to what would actually change a decision. Presented as a named, recurring empirical finding rather than a general observation.

**What it's trying to be true about.** Whether a *proposed measurement effort* is worth doing at all, given a specific pending decision it would inform — i.e., not "what is true," but "is finding out worth the cost of finding out." This is a genuinely different target than GUM/VIM (which are about the honesty of a measurement once made) — Hubbard's system operates *upstream*, at the decision of whether to measure/investigate at all.

**Uncertainty/gap handling.** This is the most directly gap-triage-shaped system found in the sweep: EVI/EOL give a formal, computable ranking for "which of our current uncertainties is worth resolving next," which is a different and complementary question to GUM's "how do I honestly quantify the uncertainty I already have." The calibrated-assessment mechanism separately targets a specific, named failure mode (systematic overconfidence in *stated* uncertainty) with a measured baseline and a trained fix — i.e., "uncertainty about uncertainty," addressed procedurally rather than just flagged.

**Feature our atom-kinds would care about.** If verisectorium atoms carry open gaps, EVI/EOL is the one system in this sweep offering a principled *ordering* over which gap to close next, rather than a static label for how uncertain something is. That's a genuinely different axis from everything else in this file — closer to "Salience" or a triage instrument than to "Evidence" — and is probably the single most transplantable idea here for the steward's phrase about explaining gaps "effectively."

## Ranking carried forward from the search pass (re-affirmed after deepening)

1. **GUM's Type A/Type B axis** — a real, internationally standardized, non-hierarchical classification of *how an uncertainty claim was produced*, orthogonal to magnitude. Directly transplantable to an Evidence/support-kind field.
2. **Hubbard's EOL/EVI/EVPI** — the only system in this sweep with a formal, computable answer to "which gap is worth resolving next," rather than a static uncertainty label.
3. **VIM's metrological traceability chain** — terminal anchor (SI) + per-link uncertainty accumulation + explicit time-stamped decay + branching for multi-input measurements. Primary-verified this session, and the branching/non-guarantee notes weren't in the original search pass at all — new information from the deepening (see below).
4. **Calibrated probability assessment (equivalent-bet training)** — an empirically-measured *specific* human-bias failure mode (default overconfidence in stated intervals) paired with a named procedural fix, i.e. a concrete answer to "uncertainty about uncertainty."
5. **Confidence vs. credible interval distinction** — forces "true about the procedure" vs. "true about the parameter given belief" apart, mapping cleanly onto Joseph's framing question.

## What surprised me / what we did not ask about

- **VIM's NOTE 5 was the most quietly important find of this whole sweep**, and it wasn't in the original search-pass summary at all: metrological traceability *explicitly does not guarantee the uncertainty is adequate for the purpose, or that there are no mistakes*. In other words, the most rigorous provenance-chain system in existence separates "this has a documented, unbroken lineage" from "this is trustworthy for what you're about to use it for" as two genuinely independent properties — you can have perfect traceability to a bad result. That's a direct challenge to any temptation in our own design to treat a well-formed provenance chain (citations resolve, DAG is acyclic, everything traces to a primary) as itself sufficient evidence of trustworthiness. It isn't, by the standard-bearer's own admission. I'd flag this as something the taxonomy's Freshness/Coverage/Evidence axes should explicitly *not* conflate: chain-integrity and fitness-for-purpose need to stay visibly separate fields, not one field standing in for the other.

- **The traceability chain can branch, not just chain linearly** (VIM NOTE 4) — a measurement with multiple input quantities requires *each* input to itself be traceable, which produces a DAG/genealogy shape rather than a single pedigree line. Our own atom-provenance thinking (citations, re-derivations) is probably already implicitly a DAG, but it's worth naming explicitly that metrology's own gold-standard system is not a chain-of-custody in the singular sense — it's a converging tree of chains, same as ours would need to be for any claim built on more than one prior.

- **GUM's explicit refusal to rank Type A above Type B** surprised me on reflection, because the intuitive folk hierarchy (measured/statistical > judgment/asserted) is exactly what I'd have expected the most rigorous body in the field to formalize — and they deliberately didn't. The stated reason (both are variances/std-devs of a probability distribution once evaluated; the letter is about *reporting provenance*, not *nature*) is a genuinely different philosophical stance than I expected walking in, and worth Joseph's attention specifically because our current organic ladders (per the taxonomy-fill draft's Evidence axis, "support-kind × strength" as a "trio cut") may be assuming a rank-order between evidence-kinds that the most established system in this space explicitly declines to assert.

- **We did not ask about, and this sweep did not surface anything on**: measurement-system analysis / repeatability-and-reproducibility (Gage R&R) as used in manufacturing quality control, which has its own well-established two-axis split (repeatability = same operator/instrument variation; reproducibility = across-operator/instrument variation) that seems structurally close kin to a "who/what produced this claim, and does it replicate across different producers" question our Accounts/Witness-Position axis might care about. Flagging as a plausible next-sweep target rather than chasing it here, since it wasn't in the assigned domain and I didn't want to thin out verification depth on the assigned systems to chase it.

- **We also did not ask about**: how metrology handles *systematic* uncertainty that is suspected but not yet quantified — i.e., "known unknown, unquantified" as its own state, separate from both Type A and Type B (which both presuppose the component has been evaluated into a number). GUM's own framework, as best I can tell from the secondaries, doesn't appear to have a first-class slot for "we know there's probably a bias here but haven't attempted to bound it" — that state seems to fall outside the budget entirely until someone does the work to evaluate it as Type B. If true, that's a real gap in GUM's own coverage worth naming rather than assuming GUM has already solved every shape of "gap" our taxonomy might want — an unquantified suspected bias is arguably a very common real-world state for a knowledge-atom to be in, and metrology's flagship system may simply not have a first-class place for it either. I could not verify this claim against GUM's primary text this session (the PDF-extraction wall again), so I'm marking it explicitly as an inference from the secondaries' silence on the topic, not a verified absence — worth a targeted follow-up read of GUM's actual clause 3 (types of uncertainty components) rather than trusting my inference here.

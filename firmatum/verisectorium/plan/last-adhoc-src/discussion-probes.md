# A plausible ungrounded claim is worse than a gap

*The interpretive layer — where a corpus explains what its results mean — is where confident-sounding claims that derive from nothing slip in. It needs its own challenge, and the cheapest one is three questions.*

## The claim

Formal or empirical content usually gets checked: a derivation is followed, a number is re-run, a citation is located. The **interpretive** layer around it — the paragraphs saying what the result means, why it matters, what it explains — routinely is not, because interpretation reads as commentary rather than as claim. It isn't. Future readers inherit it as foundation, and future work attaches to it.

The asymmetry that makes this urgent:

> A gap is a known unknown. A plausible-sounding ungrounded claim is an unknown unknown masquerading as a known known.

So an ungrounded-but-plausible explanatory claim is **worse than an omission**, not a lesser version of a good one. An omission is visible and someone eventually fills it. A confident-sounding claim that derives from nothing recruits belief in proportion to how insightful it sounds — which is exactly backwards, since sounding insightful is what got it past review.

**The probe is three questions asked of every explanatory claim**, and its virtue is that it costs one reviewer and one pass:

1. Does this **follow from the already-laid foundation** — definitions, results, whatever is upstream in the dependency chain?
2. If not, is it **labeled a hypothesis with a falsification criterion**?
3. Or is it a **plausible-sounding post-hoc explanation of nothing** — a claim that sounds like it derives from the material without connecting to it?

Read as options, those are also the three repairs: derive it properly, label it a hypothesis and name what would falsify it, or **cut it**. Cutting is honorable and usually cheapest; a clean omission beats an ungrounded claim by the asymmetry above.

Two smaller distinctions do a lot of work in practice. *"This follows from X via Y"* is grounded. *"This is consistent with Y"* is consistency, not derivation, and should say so. *"This suggests Z, though we do not derive Z"* is honest speculation — useful as scope, useless as claim.

## Why it is hard to catch from inside

The failure has a recognizable feel rather than a recognizable form. The sentence-shape *"this resolves X"* or *"this explains why Y"* arrives with the satisfaction of insight attached, and that satisfaction is precisely what suppresses the check. The reliable test is mechanical rather than intuitive: *if I had to write this as a derived result with an explicit dependency list, would it hold?*

The canonical instance is a Discussion framing — deliberation as "computation on existing data" — that **passed review unchallenged because it sounded like a deep insight.** It wasn't: it was a reductionist reframing that did not follow from the theory's foundations and in fact contradicted the theory's own counterfactual-reasoning machinery. Joseph caught it, on the substance: a chess engine exploring millions of future states is not computing on existing data in any meaningful sense. Note what the review process actually did — it passed a claim that contradicted the corpus it was reviewing, because the claim was well-shaped.

## Strength & grounds

**Formulation, in force as practice, with one worked defect.** The asymmetry argument (unknown unknown vs known unknown) is analytic and does not depend on the instance. The instance is real, documented, and caught — but it is *one* caught defect, self-reported by the corpus that caught it, and there is no measurement of how often the probes fire or how many ungrounded claims they miss. The discipline is live in ASF (`~/.claude/memory/epistemic-discipline/gate2-probes-discussion.md`; the same principle sits in its project law), which is evidence it is affordable, not evidence it is sufficient.

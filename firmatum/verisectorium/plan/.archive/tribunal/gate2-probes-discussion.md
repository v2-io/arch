# Gate 2 must probe Discussion claims, not just derivations

## The principle

Gate 2 reviews must subject **Discussion-section arguments** to the
same epistemic rigor as **Formal Expression derivations**.

Every explanatory claim in Discussion should face an epistemic
tribunal:

1. **Does this follow from the already-laid foundation** (definitions,
   derivations, results upstream in the dependency chain)?

2. **If not, is it labeled as a hypothesis** with a falsification
   criterion?

3. **Or is it a reasonable-sounding post-hoc explanation of nothing** —
   a claim that sounds insightful but doesn't actually derive from or
   connect to the formalism?

## Why this matters

**Plausible-sounding explanations that aren't grounded in the theory
are *worse* than gaps — they create false confidence.**

A gap is a known unknown. A plausible-sounding ungrounded claim is an
**unknown unknown** masquerading as a known known. Future readers
inherit it as foundation; future derivations built on it are built on
sand.

## The canonical worked example

The *"deliberation as computation on existing data"* framing **passed
Gate 2 review unchallenged because it sounded like a deep insight**.
It wasn't. It was a **reductionist reframing that didn't follow from
AAT's foundations**, contradicted the theory's own Level 3
counterfactual reasoning machinery, and was corrected by Joseph who
pointed out:

> Deliberation is **internal exploration** (simulation, synthesis,
> counterfactual reasoning), **not mere data reprocessing**. A chess
> engine exploring millions of future states is not *"computing on
> existing data"* in any meaningful sense.

The Discussion claim **sounded grounded** — *"deliberation can be seen
as computation on existing data"* — but it neither **followed from**
nor **connected to** the formalism. It was Discussion-by-association,
not Discussion-by-derivation.

## How to apply

When reviewing Discussion paragraphs, ask:

> *"Does this claim ADD something that follows from the formalism, or
> does it just SOUND like it does?"*

If the latter:

- **Derive it properly** — work through the dependency chain from
  upstream definitions / derivations and either land it as a derived
  result or surface the gap.
- **Label it as hypothesis** — with a falsification criterion. *"We
  hypothesize X; this is falsified if Y is observed."*
- **Cut it.** A clean omission is better than an ungrounded claim.

## The body-feel signal

When you read a Discussion paragraph and the sentence-shape says
*"this resolves X"* or *"this explains why Y"* — pause and ask:

- *What in the upstream dependency chain forces this?*
- *If I had to write this as `*[Derived]*` with an explicit dependency
  list, would it hold?*

If the answer to (2) is no, **the Discussion paragraph is sounding
deep without being deep**. That's the failure mode.

## Why Gate 2 is the right place for this

Gate 1 verifies formal claims; Gate 2 verifies the *interpretive layer*
around them. A theory's **Discussion sections are where the meaning
gets attached**, and that's where the ungrounded-but-plausible failure
most often slips in.

If Gate 2 only probes Formal Expression, the Discussion layer becomes
a place where authoritative-sounding speculation lives without
challenge. Future readers treat the Discussion as part of the theory
(rightly — it's in the segment); future derivations attach to it.
**The cost compounds.**

## How to write Discussion to pass this gate

- Every explanatory claim should be **traceable to an upstream node
  in the dependency chain** OR **labeled as hypothesis with a
  falsifier** OR **clearly marked as discussion-grade speculation**.
- *"This follows from segment X via the directed-separation result"*
  is grounded.
- *"This is consistent with the observation that Y"* is consistent-
  with, not derived — mark explicitly.
- *"This suggests Z, though we don't derive Z here"* is honest
  speculation — useful as scope, not as claim.
